Lidar viewer methods

How the lidar viewer draws the elevation surveys it hosts: what each layer shows, how to read it, and where it can mislead. Each section here matches a section of the viewer's sidebar.

1. The surveys

Each survey is hosted as two products: an archive Cloud-Optimized GeoTIFF in its local UTM zone (NAD83(2011)), which GIS software such as QGIS can open straight from its URL, and a web pyramid of elevation tiles that this viewer and the inventory map read. Every view here, including the shading, is computed in your browser from those elevations; nothing is pre-rendered except the optional slope ramp (below).

The footprint shown for a survey is where it has data, not its bounding box. Some surveys are restricted and appear only to signed-in collaborators. Only surveys in view limits the list to surveys whose footprint the map currently shows.

2. Basemap and lidar opacity

The basemaps are the same collection as the inventory map's, plus any shared map layers you can see there. Lidar opacity fades the whole lidar stack — shading, imagery drape, vegetation height and difference — so the basemap shows through.

3. Imagery

Offered only for surveys flown with a camera (structure-from-motion surveys): the orthomosaic from the same flight as the surface, drawn on top of everything else. A lidar terrain model has no imagery of its own.

Drop its opacity to read the two together: the shading gives the form, the imagery gives the ground cover, and at around half you can see which boulders on the hillshade are bare rock and which are vegetated.

4. Vegetation height

Shown where a survey has a surface of highest returns as well as its ground surface: the highest return in each 1 m cell minus this surface, i.e. everything the ground classification put above the ground. Switch surfaces to compare what each one removed.

Magenta marks the surface sitting above every return, which real ground cannot do: there the surface has invented a feature.

5. Layer stack: hillshade, slope, mod 5 m, aspect

The shading is computed in the browser from the elevation tiles, which is what makes the sun movable: re-lighting a slope from a different azimuth is a mapping technique, not a cosmetic setting — scarps and old failure scars appear and vanish depending on which way they are lit. Preset is hillshade composited in Hard light with the slope ramp and the mod 5 m banding; Hillshade only turns the ramps off.

  • Hillshade. Sun azimuth and altitude, a shading exaggeration (which steepens the shading, not the terrain), and how the shade combines with the ramps beneath it: Hard light (the shade decides), Overlay (the ramp decides; it fades where the ramp is pale, which is why it reads faint on moderate slopes), Multiply (shadows only) or plain transparency.
  • Slope ramp. Two deliberate sensitivities. Green to white across 0–10° resolves near-horizontal ground (benches, scarp crowns, terraces); pale from 10° to 32°; then yellow at 40°, red at 45° and purple at 50°, bracketing the angle of repose. From the archive reads slope from a pyramid pre-computed from the full-resolution survey, in true metres, where one has been built; unticked (or where none exists) slope is computed from the elevation tiles at the zoom shown, so zoomed out it reflects slope at that coarser scale.
  • Mod 5 m. Colour bands that repeat every 5 m of elevation: contours you can read as texture. The interval is fixed, not adjustable, so banding can be compared between sites.
  • Aspect tint. Colours ground by the direction it faces (hue), more strongly the steeper it is (saturation).

The colour ramps are copied verbatim from the QGIS project behind our Kachemak Bay maps, so a site rendered here matches one rendered there.

6. Difference

Colours the overlap of two surveys by how far the ground moved between them, on the ColorBrewer PRGn ramp: green where it rose, purple where it fell, neutral where it did not, and transparent outside the overlap.

Two surveys years apart are scaled as a rate, metres per year, so a pair eleven years apart and a pair one year apart can be read against each other. Two renderings of the same date are scaled in plain metres. Ramp saturates at sets the value at which the colours reach their darkest; anything faster is drawn at the end colour.

A whole survey in one flat colour is not change but a vertical datum disagreement between the two surveys — which is what this view is best at catching (see Heights and vertical datums).

7. View

North up and Nadir lock the bearing and the pitch. Keep overlapping DEMs warm preloads every survey covering the view so switching between them is instant; the line under it reports what is cached.

Go to a location. The ⌖ button under the zoom buttons takes a latitude and longitude (61.21, -149.90, or with N/S/E/W, or in degrees, minutes and seconds); pasting coordinates anywhere on the map does the same. Right-click copies the coordinates of a point, so a location copied here or on the inventory map pastes straight back.

8. 3D terrain and context

Context. Without context a survey stands on a flat plane as a block of towers. Context is USGS 3DEP (1/3 arc-second) from a pre-baked pyramid around the surveys, and it fills right up to the survey's boundary, not just the tiles the survey lacks, so the last partial tile at a survey's edge does not drop to a 0 m shelf. Water and other voids inside the boundary stay voids, at 0 m, rather than being painted with a regional model's guess at the sea surface.

In Alaska 3DEP is 2010 IfSAR, so glaciers that have since retreated still show as ice, and a newer survey steps off them with a cliff. That artifact is left in deliberately: an honest step beats a masked one.

Global terrain beyond the bake. Beyond the pre-baked pyramid the ground comes from Mapterhorn's global terrain: the same 3DEP data everywhere in Alaska (it matches the bake to a median of 0 m), and Copernicus 30 m in Canada, served from a CDN. Untick it for the bake alone, flat beyond. Shade the context hillshades that terrain beneath the surveys, because the 3D mesh on its own is too coarse to show detail.

Terrain mesh. Fast samples the same elevations through tiles twice the size, one zoom coarser: a quarter of the terrain meshes to build and draw. Fine uses the standard tiles.

Fade into the distance. Applies only once the view is pitched: looking straight down there is no far field. On a deep bathymetric survey the fade reads depth as distance and washes the seabed out past a certain contour, so turn it off there.

9. Landform traces

Lines traced on these surfaces by the inventory's editors: scarps (of any origin — not a fault map), moraine crests, trimlines, strandlines (former shorelines) and lineaments. They are working observations, not a published map. Click a line to read its note, who traced it and which surveys it was read from. Traces can belong to a named group (one glaciation, one fault), and each group can be downloaded as GeoJSON. The same traces show on the inventory map.

Group profile (∿ profile beside a group). Every trace in the group is sampled along its length for elevation and projected onto one vertical plane — for lateral moraines on both sides of a valley, the classic way to read a former ice surface. The plane runs in the group's direction of greatest horizontal extent (through the two points of the group that lie farthest apart), down the middle of the band the traces occupy; it is drawn on the map from A to A′ while the profile is open, and Flip swaps the ends. Drag either end to set the plane by hand: it then runs through the two ends as you place them (lengthened if needed to cover every trace in the group), and Auto plane returns to the automatic one. The profile redraws when a trace in the group is added, moved or removed. Each sample is read from the first surface with data under it: the surveys the trace was drawn on, then the survey on screen, then the finest other survey covering it, then Mapterhorn. A group is drawn as one line colour, whatever its number of traces. Hovering picks the nearest sample and marks it on the map with its foot on the plane; dragging across the chart gives a window whose slope is the group's trend along the plane (for moraine crests, the gradient of the former ice surface). The status line gives each surface's share. The CSV lists every sample with its distance along the plane, its perpendicular offset from it and the surface it came from. A trace far off the plane is projected all the same, so read offsets before reading a trend.

10. Heights and vertical datums

Heights are in metres, in each survey's own vertical datum; the catalogue records it per survey, nearly always NAVD88. Two cautions. NAVD88 is a land-survey datum, not local sea level: in Alaska the two differ by up to a metre or more, and by different amounts along the coast. And surveys that all say NAVD88 can still disagree by tens of centimetres to more than a metre, depending on the geoid model used to convert their GPS heights, which is exactly what a flat-coloured difference reveals.
