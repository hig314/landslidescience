How we read landslide motion from radar

Satellite radar can measure ground movement of a few millimetres per year from 700 km up. What it cannot do is tell you which direction the ground moved. This page walks through how we get from that limited measurement to a statement like "this slope is rotating downhill at half a degree per thousand years" — and, just as importantly, where that reasoning runs out.

Every 3-D figure below can be dragged to rotate. The physics is in the pictures; the algebra is there for anyone who wants it, but nothing depends on reading it.

Step 1What the radar actually measures

A radar satellite measures one thing: whether a patch of ground got closer to it or farther from it. Not north, not up — just nearer or farther along the line joining ground to satellite, the line of sight.

If the ground moves by some 3-D velocity u, the satellite records the shadow that u casts on its line of sight:

LOS rate = u · l  =  uElE + uNlN + uUlU

where l is a unit vector pointing from the ground up to the satellite. Sentinel-1 passes over twice with different geometry — once heading north (ascending, looking east, so the satellite sits to the west) and once heading south (descending, looking west, satellite to the east). Both look down at about 39° from vertical:

lasc = (−0.613, −0.142, 0.777)     ldesc = (+0.613, −0.142, 0.777)

So we get two numbers describing a motion that has three components. Drag the figure to look around; use the sliders to change how the ground is really moving and watch what the two satellites report.

The blind direction. Because both orbits are near-polar and both look sideways (east or west), neither is sensitive to motion along roughly north–south — precisely, the direction lasc × ldesc, which is north tilted 10° up. Any amount of motion along that line is invisible. Two measurements, three unknowns: one whole direction of movement is unmeasured, and no amount of processing recovers it from these two passes alone.

Step 2The radar follows the rocks, not the landscape

There is a second property of the measurement that matters as much as the first, and it is easy to get backwards. The radar echo from a patch of ground is not an even average over that patch. It is dominated by whatever within it reflects brightly — boulders, rock faces, angular blocks. The interferometer compares the travel time to those reflectors between two passes, so what it measures is the displacement of material, not the change in the shape of the landscape.

Physically: it is a Lagrangian measurement, following the rocks, rather than an Eulerian one, watching a fixed location. The distinction is invisible in most settings and decisive on a slope.

Consider material creeping downslope along a surface whose shape stays the same — a landslide in steady motion. Survey the topography twice and nothing has changed anywhere. But every rock has slid down the incline, so every rock is lower than it was, and the radar sees it:

drag to rotate
Nothing changes, and yet something moves. The hillside's shape is fixed, so the elevation at the hollow marker never changes. The marked rocks slide along the surface — and because the surface tilts, sliding along it means going down. The radar reports that descent, correctly.
vertical motion of the material  =  (elevation change of the landscape)  −  (downslope speed) × tan(slope angle)

On a 25° slope, material creeping 6 mm/yr downhill descends 2.8 mm/yr even where the topography is perfectly steady. This is the same relation glaciologists call emergence and submergence velocity, and it has two consequences we rely on:

  • Our model is already the right kind. We describe the motion of a body of material, and the radar measures the motion of material. Slope-parallel sliding enters our equations with exactly the downward component above — no correction is needed, and none is applied.
  • These rates cannot be compared with elevation-change maps. Differencing two elevation models measures how the landscape changed; radar measures how the rock moved. On a slope the two differ by the downslope term above, and a disagreement between them is not necessarily an error in either.
Where this gets fragile. The reasoning holds while the same reflectors keep dominating the echo. If bright reflectors tumble, weather, get buried, or move far enough to leave the patch, the comparison is no longer between the same objects and the measurement degrades — usually into noise, but not neutrally. Processing keeps the ground that stays consistent between passes, and ground that is deforming hard is exactly the ground that tends not to. So the parts of a landslide we can measure are biased toward its better-behaved parts, and rates from this method should be read as likely conservative. This is one reason the fast-moving core of a slide can appear as a hole in the data rather than as a peak.

Step 3One point is hopeless. A whole hillside is not.

The usual escape is to assume something — most commonly that the ground slides parallel to the slope surface. That works, but it quietly assumes the answer: you have decided the motion is downslope before measuring, so you can never afterwards use the data to test whether it is.

We take a different route. Instead of adding an assumption about one point, we use many points at once and assume they belong to the same moving body. Any rigid motion — however complicated — is a rotation plus a shift:

u(r) = ω × r + b

ω is the rotation (its direction is the axis, its length the rate) and b the overall drift. Six numbers describe the motion of the entire hillside, no matter how many points we measure. So the bookkeeping changes completely:

UnknownsMeasurementsUnmeasured directions
Each point on its own3 n2 n n — one per point
Points sharing one rigid motion62 n 1 — no matter how large n is

That is the whole idea. We buy the third dimension with spatial coherence rather than with an assumption about what drives the landslide — which leaves gravity available afterwards as something to check, instead of something we built in. One unmeasured direction still remains, so we always report how far our conclusions could move along it.

Step 4Why rotation survives when everything else fails

There is a second, less obvious gain, and it does a lot of work later. Compare what the two ingredients of rigid motion look like across a hillside:

  • A drift moves every point identically — it writes a constant offset into the measurements.
  • A rotation moves distant points faster than near ones — it writes a gradient, a systematic change from one end of the slope to the other.

Because those two signatures are different shapes, a single satellite pass can tell them apart, even though a single pass can never recover the full 3-D motion. Switch between the modes and watch the plot beneath the slope:

Flat line versus tilted line. Each coloured line is what one satellite would record walking from the head of the slide to its toe. Drift raises or lowers the line; rotation tilts it. Because the tilt is what carries the rotation, we can follow a slope's rotation rate through years when only one of the two satellites was observing.

Step 5Bringing gravity in — as a test, not an assumption

A landslide is driven by gravity, so its motion should have a particular geometry. We make that expectation concrete before looking at the answer:

  • Fit a plane to the hillside and find its dropline — the direction water would run.
  • A block slumping under gravity rotates about a horizontal axis lying across the slope, perpendicular to that dropline. Call it the expected pole.
  • The physical requirement is simply that the block's centre of mass gets lower.

That last point is worth dwelling on, because it is where intuition often misfires. Rotate the block below and watch the head, the toe, and the centre of mass separately:

drag to rotate
Falling downhill while part of you goes up. As the block rotates, the head drops and the toe rises — and the centre of mass falls the whole time. So measured uplift at the toe of a landslide is not a contradiction and not a data error; it is what a slump looks like. Note that we do not test this by averaging the vertical motion — Step 7 explains why that average is not something this data can pin down. We test it through the rotation rate itself.

Step 6Reading the pictures — and one trap in them

Geologists conventionally plot directions on a stereonet: imagine every direction as an arrow from the centre of a sphere, keep only the lower half, and flatten it to a disc. A steeply plunging direction lands near the middle; a horizontal one lands on the rim.

The convention has a catch that cost us a genuine misreading of our own results. Only the lower hemisphere is drawn, so an upward direction is flipped through the centre and plotted at its opposite. Under a rotation, the head and the toe move in exactly opposite directions — so they land on the same spot:

Why the stereonet cannot show a slump. The head arrow and the toe arrow are equal and opposite. The upward one is folded through the origin (dashed) and lands on top of the other, distinguishable only by being drawn hollow. The up-and-down structure — the actual evidence of rotation — is invisible.

So on the results pages we plot the rotation two further ways: as a downslope profile of vertical rate, which shows head-down/toe-up directly, and with the overall drift removed, because the drift is often as large as the rotation and swamps it in any direction plot.

Step 7What this can and cannot determine

Real hillsides are not rigid — they stretch, compress and shear internally. A more honest description of the velocity field allows all of that:

u(r) = Ar + b     A = (rotation) + (stretching & shearing)

That is twelve numbers. But each satellite pass only ever reveals four of them (the combination l·A, which is three numbers, plus l·b). Two passes therefore pin down eight of twelve — and the missing four straddle the boundary between rotation and internal deformation. The two are entangled in what we can see.

The consequence, stated plainly. If we assume a hillside is rigid, the fit will hand back a rotation axis with a tight-looking uncertainty — even when the real motion was mostly internal stretching that got mathematically reclassified as rotation. In our own Matanuska data, allowing internal deformation swings the recovered axis by 20–60° while barely changing how well the model fits. The direction of the rotation axis is therefore not something two satellites can determine on a deforming slope, and we do not report it as one.

What survives is more modest, and well posed. "How fast is this slope rotating about the axis gravity predicts?" is a one-number question with a real answer and a real uncertainty. "What is this slope's rotation axis?" is not answerable. So we ask the first question, and we report the second as undetermined whenever the test above says it is.

Why we do not report whether the mass is falling

The unmeasured direction from Step 1 turns out to be pure sideways drift — it contains no rotation whatsoever. That single fact sorts our quantities into trustworthy and untrustworthy, and not the way we first assumed.

Because that invisible direction is tilted about 10° above horizontal, drift we cannot detect carries vertical motion we cannot detect. The domain-average vertical rate therefore slides along with it — at Matanuska it ranges from +0.8 to −6.4 mm/yr across a plausible range of invisible drift, moving 0.18 mm/yr for every 1 mm/yr of drift. "Is the mass falling?" has no answer from two look directions.

The rotation rate about a specified axis is, by contrast, exactly unchanged by the same shift — a uniform drift has no rotation in it to contribute, so it cannot alter a rotation rate. That invariance, not the vertical average, is what makes Ω the quantity we report.

The short version of Steps 1, 2 and 7. Two satellites give two numbers about a three-dimensional motion of material. Averages over the missing direction are not recoverable; gradients across a hillside are. So we build every conclusion on how motion changes from one end of a landslide to the other, and treat any quantity that depends on the overall drift as undetermined.

Step 8Rotation changes over time

A single rate averaged over nine years can describe a state the slope was never in. We therefore re-fit the rotation in overlapping two-year windows, holding the geometry fixed and letting only the rate vary, which produces a rotation-rate history rather than a number.

This is also where Step 3 earns its keep. The two satellites did not observe the same years — at our sites ascending runs 2016–2020 and descending 2018–2025, with only about two years of overlap. Because rotation shows up as a gradient, a single track can still track it, so the history spans the whole record. Where both exist, we fit each alone as a cross-check: two independent viewing geometries agreeing is the strongest statement this data can make.

A caution that applies to every number on the results pages. Any 3-D fit that uses each satellite's full-record average is mixing different years together. Every element therefore also carries a common-window refit using only the overlap, and where the two disagree, the overlap version is the one to trust for anything three-dimensional.

Step 9Choosing what counts as one moving body

Everything above assumes we know which points belong together. We grow candidate domains outward from the strongest signal, adding neighbours while each one stays consistent with a single rigid motion — deliberately ignoring the mapped landslide outline, so that agreement or disagreement with the map is a result rather than an input.

A failure we walked into. Growing a domain by minimising misfit prefers small, tidy patches. But a rotation only reveals itself as a change from one end of the slide to the other — so a patch too short to span head-to-toe cannot express rotation at all. At Matanuska the neatly-grown pieces returned "no rotation," while the full mapped extent — a visibly worse rigid fit — returned a clear rotation, lowering mass, at three times its uncertainty. Optimising for tidiness destroyed the signal. We now always analyse the mapped extent alongside the grown ones and report each domain's downslope length, so this is visible rather than hidden.

Step 10What we still owe

  • Calibration against noise. A procedure that searches for well-fitting regions will find them in pure noise too. Until we run the identical search on randomised fields and learn what it turns up there, significance values from the search should be read as provisional.
  • The unmeasured direction. One direction of motion is never observed. We quote how far each conclusion could shift along it; where a conclusion flips inside that range, we say so instead of choosing.
  • Point sampling only. These results come from sampled points rather than the full-resolution radar imagery, and spatial correlation between nearby points is measured and accounted for, not assumed.
